Learning a Scene with 3D Gaussian Splatting

Three photographs, one shared 3D representation

By Edward Storey,Alec Helbling

September 28, 2026

Introduction

A central goal in computer vision and graphics is novel view synthesis: given images of a scene captured from known camera positions, produce convincing images from new viewpoints. A practical scene representation must be learned efficiently from those observed images, then deployed to render novel views with low latency. 3D Gaussian splatting has emerged as a highly efficient, state-of-the-art method for representing and rendering 3D scenes [?] . It represents a scene as a collection of learnable, spatially distributed 3D Gaussians that can be projected, or “splatted,” onto an image and composited to produce a rendered view.

This article examines not only what Gaussian splatting is, but why it is such an effective representation for real-time rendering. Our central argument is that Gaussian splatting succeeds through the co-design of the scene representation and its rendering algorithm. We first explain how Gaussians represent a scene and how splatting produces an image. We then discuss how this representation is learned from observed views. Finally, we work through the design of an efficient GPU renderer, showing how this co-design produces a highly parallel pipeline. We implement the renderer in WebGPU so that different design decisions and their effects on performance can be compared directly in the browser.

Splatting a Gaussian

Camera model. We use a calibrated pinhole camera. Its extrinsic parameters R\mathbf{R}R and t\mathbf{t}t transform a point from world space into the camera's coordinate system. Its intrinsic parameters—the focal lengths fx,fyf_x, f_yfx​,fy​ and principal point (cx,cy)(c_x,c_y)(cx​,cy​)—then map camera-space coordinates onto image pixels. We write this projection function as π\piπ.

  • First, transform its mean from world space into camera space.
    μc=Rμ+t=[xyz]\boldsymbol{\mu}_c = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{t} = \begin{bmatrix} x \\ y \\ z \end{bmatrix}μc​=Rμ+t=​xyz​​
  • A perspective camera projects that point by dividing its horizontal and vertical coordinates by its depth.
    π(x,y,z)=[fxx/z+cxfyy/z+cy]\pi(x,y,z) = \begin{bmatrix} f_x x/z + c_x \\ f_y y/z + c_y \end{bmatrix}π(x,y,z)=[fx​x/z+cx​fy​y/z+cy​​]
  • Because of the division by zzz, perspective projection is nonlinear. We cannot project the Gaussian's covariance with one exact linear transformation.
  • Instead, approximate the projection locally around the Gaussian's camera-space mean using a first-order Taylor expansion.
    π(x)≈π(μc)+J(x−μc)\pi(\mathbf{x}) \approx \pi(\boldsymbol{\mu}_c) + \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_c)π(x)≈π(μc​)+J(x−μc​)
  • The Jacobian is the local linear description of the camera projection at that mean.
    J=∂π∂x∣μc=[fxz0−fxxz20fyz−fyyz2]\mathbf{J} = \left.\frac{\partial \pi}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{\mu}_c} = \begin{bmatrix} \dfrac{f_x}{z} & 0 & -\dfrac{f_xx}{z^2} \\[6pt] 0 & \dfrac{f_y}{z} & -\dfrac{f_yy}{z^2} \end{bmatrix}J=∂x∂π​​μc​​=​zfx​​0​0zfy​​​−z2fx​x​−z2fy​y​​​
  • Core assumption: Over the extent of one Gaussian, perspective projection is assumed to be well approximated by this local linearization around its mean. The projected footprint is not an exact 2D Gaussian; the approximation is effective when each Gaussian covers a sufficiently local region of space.
  • Rotate the world-space covariance into camera space.
    Σc=RΣRT\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c = \mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{T}Σc​=RΣRT
  • Because a Gaussian remains Gaussian under a linear transformation, use the Jacobian to project the covariance into image space.
    Σ2D=JΣcJT=JRΣRTJT\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D} = \mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_c\mathbf{J}^{T} = \mathbf{J}\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{R}^{T}\mathbf{J}^{T}Σ2D​=JΣc​JT=JRΣRTJT
  • The resulting 2D Gaussian is centered at
    μ2D=π(Rμ+t)\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2D} = \pi(\mathbf{R}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\mathbf{t})μ2D​=π(Rμ+t)
    and its shape is determined by Σ2D\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{2D}Σ2D​. This is the splat that will be blended into the image [?] .

Optimizing Through the Renderer

Keeping covariances valid.

  • A covariance matrix must remain symmetric and positive semidefinite.
  • Optimizing its entries directly can produce an invalid covariance.
  • Instead, optimize a scale and rotation, then construct the covariance as
    Σ=RSSTRT\boldsymbol{\Sigma} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{S}\mathbf{S}^{T}\mathbf{R}^{T}Σ=RSSTRT
  • Constrain the scales to be positive and represent the rotation with a normalized quaternion.
  • This construction keeps the covariance valid throughout optimization.
